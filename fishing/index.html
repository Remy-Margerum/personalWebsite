Fetching the water…

Pacific bluefin are the cold-tolerant tuna — caught off California from the mid-50s into the low 70s °F. The number that matters isn’t the absolute temperature but the edge: the break where warm bight water bumps against cooler upwelled water. Fish stack on those breaks, usually on the cool side, and they follow bait more than they follow a thermometer. The gap between the black and red lines above is the highest-percentage water on the chart.

A · Santa Rosa Flats. Closest option and the one to try first for a day trip — the break tends to sit right over the shelf edge south of the northern islands, exactly where the fleet has been finding fish “sliding farther north.”

B · Osborn Bank. Classic bluefin structure below Santa Barbara Island with a real track record. When the chart shows it inside 70°F-plus water it’s the warm-side gamble — worth it only if the break has moved east.

C · San Nicolas Island. The long run and overnight territory, but the spot that keeps producing confirmed counts. If the break wraps the back of the island, this is where the proven fish are.

Working rule: find the 64–68°F break over structure, then look for bait, birds, breezing fish, and shiners. Hover the chart to read the water anywhere; the dashed rings are 20, 40, and 60 nm from the harbor mouth, and runs and bearings (magnetic, var 12.5°E) are computed great-circle from the harbor. Scrub back through the week of NOAA analyses to watch the break move, or forward past the latest analysis: forward days switch to an ocean-model forecast, drawn with dashed isotherms — coarser than the 5 km analysis, so trust the break’s trend more than its exact position. The wind layer follows the day you pick (midday, or right now for the current view). Check current CDFW bluefin limits and know the Channel Islands sanctuary and MPA boundaries before you go. A chart for planning, not navigation.
